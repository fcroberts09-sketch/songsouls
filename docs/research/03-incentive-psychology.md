# Paying for crowd contributions: what the evidence says, and what to ship

Date: 2026-09-22. Method: ~45 web searches + primary-source fetches (search budget exhausted at the end; a few paywalled PDFs returned 403, so some exact figures are labelled Likely rather than Certain).

## 1. Motivation crowding-out: the core lab and field evidence

**F1. Small tangible rewards reduce intrinsic engagement; the effect is robust across 128 experiments.** Deci (1971) paid one group ~$1 per Soma-cube puzzle solved; in a later unpaid "free-choice" period the paid group spent significantly less time on puzzles than the never-paid group. Deci, Koestner & Ryan's 1999 meta-analysis (128 studies) found engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent tangible rewards all significantly undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation, while positive verbal feedback *enhanced* it and unexpected, non-contingent rewards did no harm. Sources: https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/1971_Deci.pdf ; https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/Articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf . Confidence: Certain.

**F2. Frey & Jegen (2001) formalize the boundary conditions.** Crowding-out happens when an intervention is perceived as *controlling*; crowding-in when it is perceived as *supportive/acknowledging* (autonomy, competence, recognition). Their survey covers lab, field and econometric evidence for both directions. Source: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1467-6419.00150 . Confidence: Certain.

**F3. "Pay enough or don't pay at all" — the relationship is non-monotonic.** Gneezy & Rustichini (QJE 2000): 160 students, 50 IQ-type questions, paid 0 / 0.1 / 1 / 3 NIS per correct answer. Mean correct: ~28 (no pay), ~23 (0.1 NIS, *worse than free*), ~35 (1 NIS), ~34 (3 NIS). Door-to-door charity collection by high-schoolers: unpaid group collected the most (~239 NIS), 1%-commission group collected ~154, 10%-commission group ~219 — still below unpaid. Conclusion: tiny pay converts a social act into a badly-paid job; once you pay, more is better. Source: https://rady.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty-research/uri-gneezy/pay-enough.pdf ; https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v115y2000i3p791-810..html . Confidence: Certain on the pattern; Likely on the exact means (PDF was compressed, figures from memory of the paper).

**F4. "A fine is a price": prices permanently replace norms.** Gneezy & Rustichini (J. Legal Studies 2000), 10 Haifa day-cares: introducing a ~NIS 10 late-pickup fine in 6 centres roughly *doubled* late pickups; removing the fine did not restore the old behaviour. Source: https://rady.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty-research/uri-gneezy/fine.pdf . Confidence: Certain.

**F5. Titmuss was partly right: cash halves female blood donation, but a charity option undoes it.** Mellström & Johannesson (JEEA 2008) field experiment, SEK 50 (~$7): women's willingness to donate fell from ~52% to ~30% with cash; men unaffected; letting donors give the SEK 50 to charity fully restored supply (~53%). Source: https://academic.oup.com/jeea/article/6/4/845/2295788 ; https://ideas.repec.org/p/hhs/gunwpe/0180.html . Confidence: Certain on direction; Likely on the exact percentages.

**F6. But in-kind and large incentives crowd *in* at scale.** Lacetera, Macis & Slonim (AEJ: Policy 2012; ~14,000 Red Cross drives, ~100,000 donors, 72 Ohio drives with $5–$15 gift cards): incentives raised donations in 16 of 17 items tested, increasing with value, without raising ineligible-donor rates — though a large share was *displacement* from neighbouring unpaid drives. A surprise (unannounced) reward reduced later donation. Sources: https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.4.1.186 ; https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/impactevaluations/when-field-studies-diverge-lab-experiments-case-incentivized-blood-donations-guest-post-mario-macis . Confidence: Certain.

**F7. Social vs market norms (Ariely).** Heyman & Ariely (Psych Sci 2004) reported that unpaid ("social market") effort exceeded low-cash effort, cash was magnitude-sensitive and candy was not; AARP lawyers refused $30/hr but volunteered for free. Caveat: a 2021 Expression of Concern was issued (original data lost), and a 2022 Collabra replication found "rewarding more is better," more so for cash than goods — i.e. the monotonic cash effect replicated better than the "free beats cheap" result. Sources: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.0956-7976.2004.00757.x ; https://danariely.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/bauer-ariely-2021-expression-of-concern-effort-for-payment-a-tale-of-two-markets.pdf ; https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/8/1/32572/120262/ . Confidence: Certain on the concern/replication existence; Likely on replication detail.

**Boundary conditions (synthesis, Certain):** pay works when (a) it is large enough to be a real wage, (b) the task is already framed as a job/market, (c) it is in-kind, symbolic, or routed to a cause rather than cash-to-self, (d) it is non-contingent/unexpected, or (e) the population has weak intrinsic motivation. Pay backfires when it is small, cash, contingent, and layered onto a prosocial or reciprocal frame.

## 2. Evidence from real platforms

**F8. Wikipedia: fun predicts output; ideology is stated but doesn't predict output.** Nov (CACM 2007), 151 editors: Fun and Ideology ranked top, but only Fun correlated with hours contributed. Kuznetsov (SIGCAS 2006) identifies altruism, reciprocity, community and reputation as the operative values. Sources: https://cacm.acm.org/research/what-motivates-wikipedians/ ; https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1215942.1215943 . Confidence: Certain.

**F9. Symbolic recognition has large causal effects on volunteer output and retention.** Restivo & van de Rijt (PLOS ONE 2012) randomly gave barnstars to top-1% editors: +60% productivity and 6x likelihood of receiving further barnstars. Gallus (Mgmt Sci 2017) RCT on ~4,000 German-Wikipedia newcomers: purely symbolic awards raised newcomer retention by roughly 20% in the following quarter, persisting four quarters. Sources: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0034358 ; https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2016.2540 . Confidence: Certain (Restivo); Likely on the 20% figure (Gallus; paywalled).

**F10. Badges steer behaviour, then effort drops after attainment; reputation gets gamed.** Anderson et al. (WWW 2013) on Stack Overflow: activity rises approaching a badge threshold and falls after. Mao et al. (2021) documented four reputation-fraud types incl. voting rings; 60–80% of algorithmically flagged users had reputation removed by SO. Sources: https://www.cs.cornell.edu/home/kleinber/www13-badges.pdf ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.07101 . Confidence: Certain.

**F11. Status incentives distort *what* gets reviewed.** Yelp Elite: status gain triples review output relative to the loss effect of demotion, but elite-seeking over-represents high-price/unique restaurants (PLOS ONE 2021) and review quality drops after badge loss (UB 2024, 6M reviews). Sources: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0252157 ; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167811622000519 . Confidence: Certain.

**F12. Paid microtasks: money buys quantity, not quality; framing buys quality.** Mason & Watts (HCOMP 2009): higher piece rates on MTurk raised output but not accuracy (anchoring); quota schemes beat piece rates. Rogstadius et al. (ICWSM 2011): charity framing improved accuracy; pay improved uptake; the two were *additive*, not crowding. Shaw, Horton & Chen (CSCW 2011): of 14 conditions, only Bayesian Truth Serum and punishment-for-disagreement improved rater quality (~0.5 SD, p≤.05); most financial and social nudges did nothing. Ho et al. (WWW 2015, 2,000+ workers): performance bonuses help only on "effort-responsive" tasks and only when the bonus is salient; over-ambitious thresholds reduce effort. Sources: https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1600150.1600175 ; https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/view/14105 ; https://cyber.harvard.edu/sites/cyber.harvard.edu/files/Shaw-Horton-Chen_Designing_Incentives_Inexpert_Human_Raters_2011.pdf ; https://www.jennwv.com/papers/pbp.pdf . Confidence: Certain.

**F13. OSM/Waze/GasBuddy run on non-cash loops.** OSM survey of 444 mappers (Budhathoki & Haythornthwaite 2013): "personal but shared need," community, learning; casual mappers driven by free-data principles. Waze: points/ranks plus direct self-benefit, ~18M map edits/month (industry case studies, not RCTs). GasBuddy gets 2–3M price reports/day using points → raffle tickets for $100 gas cards, leaderboards, geofenced reporting, and official feeds blended in. Sources: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0002764212469364 ; https://d3.harvard.edu/platform-digit/submission/waze-traffic-from-the-crowd/ ; https://aiinstitute.hbs.edu/platform-digit/submission/gasbuddy-a-platform-for-crowdsourced-gas-price-data/ . Confidence: Likely (case-study grade).

## 3. Fraud and gaming under paid contribution

**F14. MTurk 2018 "bot" crisis was mostly VPS-based foreign fraud, ~20–27% of respondents.** Kennedy et al. (PSRM 2020): 26.9% flagged (19.3% VPS, 6.9% foreign IP); 23.9% of VPS users failed quality checks vs 2.8% of valid respondents; treatment effects attenuated ~13% at 17% contamination; 95% approval ratings no longer screened. Chmielewski & Kucker (2020) showed failed validity checks and failed replications during/after summer 2018. Kittur, Chi & Suh (CHI 2008): make cheating cost as much as honest work; require verifiable questions. Sources: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/political-science-research-and-methods/article/shape-of-and-solutions-to-the-mturk-quality-crisis/521AEEB9A9753D5C6038440BD123826C ; https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1948550619875149 ; https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1357054.1357127 . Confidence: Certain.

**F15. Receipt-reward apps face structural fraud; disclosed but unquantified.** Ibotta's FY2024 10-K states third parties "have" submitted counterfeit receipts, taken over accounts and stacked offers, and that Ibotta may have to reimburse brands; Fetch publicly acknowledges fake-receipt generators, multi-account and referral-bonus fraud and runs a dedicated anti-fraud team; both platforms rely on ML receipt scanning, permanent bans, referral-fraud detection and payout minimums ($20 on Ibotta). Sources: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1538379/000162828025008240/ibta-20241231.htm ; https://fetch.com/blog/fetch-tips-tricks/the-funniest-fake-receipts-weve-seen-at-fetch-rewards ; https://imply.io/blog/combating-fraud-at-ibotta-with-imply/ . Confidence: Certain that fraud is material; no public fraud *rate* exists.

**F16. Survey panels: ~30% of opt-in respondents fail attention checks; bogus respondents bias *upward*.** CloudResearch and Pew data show opt-in/crowdsourced panels far more likely to submit bogus data than address-recruited ones; speeding and attention checks miss most fraud. Source: https://www.cloudresearch.com/resources/blog/the-truth-about-online-data-quality/ . Confidence: Likely.

**Mitigations with evidence:** verifiable "gold" items (Kittur), geofencing at capture (GasBuddy), IP/VPS screening at entry (Kennedy — 85 blocked, 1 contested), delayed/thresholded payout (Ibotta $20), photo + GPS stamping (Premise), quota rather than piece-rate pay (Mason & Watts), and making cheating as slow as honesty.

## 4. Give-to-get gates

**F17. Glassdoor's gate reduces polarization without collapsing volume.** Marinescu, Klein, Chamberlain & Smart (NBER w24372, 2018; 116k reviews 2013–16 vs matched controls): give-to-get cut 1-star reviews by 3.6 pp and 5-star by 2.1 pp, raised 3- and 4-star by 2.6/2.9 pp. Their MTurk RCT: both relatively high cash and a prosocial cue reduced selection bias; higher pay produced longer reviews. Glassdoor still runs the policy. Sources: https://www.nber.org/papers/w24372 ; https://www.glassdoor.com/blog/give-to-get/ . Confidence: Certain. Notably, nothing published quantifies "junk submissions to pass the gate"; the rating-distribution shift toward moderate suggests the gate pulls in low-motivation (not fake) reviewers.

**F18. Blind uses a threshold, not a per-user gate:** 30 verified employees before a company channel unlocks; work-email verification is the quality mechanism. Source: https://www.worklife.news/technology/blind-anonymous-workplace/ . Confidence: Likely.

**F19. "Pay with a tweet"/share-to-unlock:** only marketer case studies (HubSpot; Twitter reported 34 organic per 100 paid impressions); no peer-reviewed quality evidence. Source: https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/use-pay-with-a-tweet-generate-leads-ht . Confidence: Guessing on effect size.

## 5. Framing of savings and fairness

**F20. Comparison to what *other consumers* paid is the most salient unfairness cue.** Xia, Monroe & Cox (J. Marketing 2004) framework; Haws & Bearden (JCR 2006) three studies: consumer-based price differences hurt fairness and satisfaction more than time-based ones. Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler (AER 1986): 82% rated the post-blizzard snow-shovel price rise unfair; dual-entitlement principle. Fehr & Schmidt (QJE 1999): disadvantageous inequality is weighted more heavily than advantageous (alpha ≥ beta). Sources: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmkg.68.4.1.42733 ; https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/33/3/304/1891884 ; https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Justice/Kahneman.pdf ; https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/114/3/817/1848113 . Confidence: Certain.

**F21. Real backlashes confirm it.** Amazon's Sept 2000 random DVD price test (68 titles, 20–40% discounts) ended with an apology and refunds averaging $3.10 to 6,896 customers. Turow et al. (Annenberg 2005, n=1,500) found a majority believed price customization was illegal or should be. Uber surge is rated unfair by a majority of riders in surveys. Sources: https://www.cnn.com/2000/TECH/computing/09/28/amazon.reut/ ; https://repository.upenn.edu/items/bd5ac24d-3f6e-44ac-b08f-ce01bb62dec5 ; https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-science-behind-behavior/201512/why-do-consumers-hate-uber-s-surge-pricing . Confidence: Certain.

**F22. Loss vs gain framing of savings.** Meta-analytic and field evidence: loss-framed benefit messages beat gain frames for credit-card uptake; a two-field-experiment coupon study (N=73,010) found beneficiary framing ("save for X") increased printing and redemption; a discount frame is judged fairer than an equivalent surcharge frame. No study tests "you overpaid by $X" in a price-comparison app specifically. Sources: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11129-023-09261-0 ; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0969698924002480 . Confidence: Likely (direction), Guessing (magnitude in this context).

## 6. Network-density loops

**F23. Product-as-reward referral loops work when the reward is the product.** Dropbox's two-sided 500 MB referral (Sept 2008) took signups from 100k to 4M in 15 months, ~35% of daily signups from referrals. Nextdoor requires 10 verified households within 21 days or the neighborhood expires; Blind requires 30. Sources: https://www.prefinery.com/blog/dropbox-referral-program-3900percent-growth-study/ ; https://help.nextdoor.com/s/article/Create-a-new-neighborhood-on-Nextdoor . Confidence: Likely (company-reported, not causal).

## Recommended incentive design, ranked by evidence strength

Context: every price check is automatically a contribution; no accounts; no affiliate revenue. This is the *best possible* setting for intrinsic + reciprocity: the marginal cost of contributing is zero, so no pay is needed to overcome effort cost (the thing pay is good at, F6/F12), and the product's value rises with density (F23).

1. **(a) No pay, intrinsic + reciprocity — SHIP FIRST.** Strongest evidence: F1–F5, F8–F9, F13. Implement the *supportive* side of Frey-Jegen: immediate self-benefit ("your check just updated 3 nearby prices"), non-contingent symbolic recognition (streaks, "first to report" badges — but avoid threshold badges that cause post-attainment drop, F10), and prosocial framing at capture (F12: framing raises accuracy at zero cost). Show contributors the aggregate effect of their data (F9 mechanism).

2. **(f) Subscription with free tier — SHIP SECOND, revenue only.** No crowding risk because it is *users paying you* (market frame on consumption, not on contribution). Keep contribution untouched by the paywall: never gate contribution behind payment, and do not let paid users skip contributing, or you reintroduce a market frame. Evidence: indirect (F2, F4).

3. **(b) Give-to-get gate — SHIP LATER, only as a soft density threshold.** Glassdoor evidence (F17) is strong that gates reduce selection bias and don't destroy volume, but there the gate solves a *selection* problem (only angry people review). Your contributions are automatic, so a per-user gate adds nothing except friction; a Blind/Nextdoor-style *area* threshold ("this store unlocks at 10 reports") is the version that maps to your problem (F18, F23). Risk to watch: junk-to-pass-gate is undocumented, not disproven.

4. **(e) Paid "coverage missions" for specific items — MAYBE, narrowly.** This is the Lacetera/Premise/GasBuddy model (F6, F13): targeted, task-framed, in-kind or raffle-style, capped. Evidence says it raises supply where intrinsic motivation is absent (new city, rare SKU), that displacement is real, and that fraud follows money (F14–F15). If used: in-kind or lottery not cash, geofenced, delayed payout, gold-item verification, and never on the main flow.

5. **(c) Small cash per submission — NEVER SHIP.** Directly contradicted by F3 (tiny pay is worse than free), F4 (irreversible), F5 (cash halves prosocial supply), F12 (cash buys quantity not quality), and it invites the receipt-app fraud economy (F15) with no accounts to ban. It also converts your zero-cost automatic contribution into a wage claim.

6. **(d) Share-of-savings kickback to the contributor whose price you used — NEVER SHIP.** No direct evidence exists, and every adjacent finding is negative: it is contingent cash (F1), it creates an "attribution lottery" that rewards fabricated low prices (a fake $0.99 report gets used by everyone — F14 logic), it requires accounts and payout rails you don't have, and inequity aversion (F20) means contributors will feel *under*-paid relative to what you or others gain. The one salvageable fragment: a *symbolic* version ("your report saved 42 people $3.10 this week") captures the reciprocity motive without cash.

**Framing of savings:** use the other-consumer comparison ("people nearby paid $X less") because it is the most salient fairness cue (F20), but frame it as an *actionable gain* for the user rather than an accusation against the store; the Amazon/Uber episodes (F21) show that "someone else paid less" reliably produces anger — direct that anger toward opening the app, not toward distrusting your data.
