# Detecting personalized / surveillance pricing: literature, investigations, regulation, and why detection was never productized

Research date: 2026-09-22. Web search budget was exhausted at ~200 queries; PDFs of the four foundational papers, Instacart's rebuttal and the NY AG letter were extracted locally (pymupdf) and quoted directly.

## 1. Findings — foundational academic papers

**F1. Mikians et al., HotNets 2012 ("Detecting price and search discrimination on the Internet").** Method: scripted crawls from 6 vantage points plus two "trained personas" (affluent vs. budget-conscious, built by browsing trained sites) and varied OS/browser; ~200 products across airlines, cameras, hotels/travel (appendix lists aa.com, amazon.com, bestbuy.com, cheaptickets.com, expedia.com, etc.). Findings, quoted: "no evidence of price/search discrimination for system based differences"; location-based differences "primarily on digital products, up to 166%—e-books and video games"; office-supply prices differed across locations within Massachusetts; "prices of products that were shown to be up to 4 times higher for affluent than for budget conscious customers" (search discrimination); arriving via a discount aggregator gave prices "23% lower." Self-stated limitation: "we cannot claim with certainty that these differences are due to price discrimination, since digital rights costs or competition could offer alternative interpretations." Conclusion floats "a watchdog system that helps users check if they are being discriminated." URL: https://conferences.sigcomm.org/hotnets/2012/papers/hotnets12-final94.pdf — Certain.

**F2. Mikians et al., CoNEXT 2013 ("Crowd-assisted search…: first results") and the $heriff extension.** Method: crowd. A browser extension let users highlight a price; the URI was re-fetched from "14 vantage points around the world," prices extracted and returned. Scale: "340 different users from 18 countries" checking "products from 600 domains"; follow-up systematic crawl of 21 retailers, up to 100 products each, daily for a week, "188K extracted prices." Findings: variation "10%–30%" for most retailers, extreme cases ×2; cheapest products showed highest variation (×3); Finland consistently most expensive location. Stated obstacles: per-site parsing templates that do not scale; noise from "A/B testing, timing difference"; confounding by "taxation, logistics, shipping costs"; controlling "physical location, system issues, and browsing history." URLs: https://arxiv.org/abs/1307.4531 ; https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2535372.2535415 ; extension site http://www.sheriff-v2.dynu.net/ — Certain. The tool is dead: a 2026 GitHub README for a successor notes "nobody has kept that tool alive" (https://github.com/Tumub/natural-price) — Likely.

**F3. Hannak, Soeller, Lazer, Mislove, Wilson, IMC 2014.** Method: both crowd and scripted. Cookies/accounts of "over 300 real-world users" (100 AMT workers each for retail, hotels, rental cars) routed through a proxy so real-user, comparison and control queries hit the site simultaneously; PhantomJS synthetic personas varying browser/OS, logged-in status, click and purchase history (they actually booked rooms/cars). 16 sites: Best Buy, CDW, Home Depot, JCPenney, Macy's, Newegg, Office Depot, Sears, Staples, Walmart, Booking, Cheaptickets, Expedia, Hotels.com, Kayak, Orbitz, Priceline, Travelocity, Venere. Findings (quoted): "eight of these sites implement personalization"; "Cheaptickets and Orbitz implement price discrimination by offering reduced prices on hotels to 'members'" (~$12 cheaper on ~5% of inventory); "Expedia and Hotels.com engage in A/B testing that steers a subset of users towards more expensive hotels"; "Home Depot and Travelocity personalize search results for users on mobile devices"; "Priceline personalizes search results based on a user's history of clicks and purchases." Magnitudes were small (Home Depot ~6%/~$0.41 per product; Travelocity ~$15 discounts for iOS). Key methodological contribution: control-twin accounts to measure baseline noise; "we cannot declare any particular inconsistency to be due to personalization (or noise), but we can report the overall rate." URLs: https://personalization.ccs.neu.edu/static/pdf/imc151-hannak.pdf ; https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2663716.2663744 — Certain.

**F4. Vissers, Nikiforakis, Bielova, Joosen, HotPETs 2014 ("Crying Wolf?").** Scripted: 25 airlines, 66 user profiles, 2 locations (US, Argentina), twice daily for 21 days, ~130,000 queries. Result: "we do not find any evidence for systematic price discrimination"; the one systematic gap was explained by Argentina's 20% tax on card purchases (from March 18, 2013). Noted confounders: fares "extremely volatile" even at ~1-minute spacing; price caching; regional fare rules (Southwest data withheld from aggregators; Peru resident fares); currency. Limitations: queries run sequentially not in parallel to avoid rate limiting; single IP per location; only two locations. URL: https://petsymposium.org/2014/papers/Vissers.pdf — Certain.

**F5. Chen, Mislove, Wilson.** "Peeking Beneath the Hood of Uber" (IMC 2015): 43 emulated Uber clients in SF and Manhattan for 4 weeks; surge active 57% of time in SF, 14% in Manhattan, most surges <10 minutes; measurement reverse-engineered surge zones, not per-user personalization (https://conferences.sigcomm.org/imc/2015/papers/p495.pdf). "An Empirical Analysis of Algorithmic Pricing on Amazon Marketplace" (WWW 2016): 500+ sellers detected using repricing algorithms; Buy Box handles 82% of sales; focus is seller-side algorithmic pricing, not buyer-specific prices (https://mislove.org/publications/Amazon-WWW.pdf) — Certain.

**F6. Hupperich, Tatang, Wilkop, Holz, CODASPY 2018.** Built PDFuzz, "an automated price scanner capable of disguising itself as an arbitrary system, leveraging real-world system fingerprints" (PhantomJS/GhostDriver), varying OS, browser, language, location. Abstract cites prior cases (office-supply location pricing, Apple-vs-Windows hotel prices). Code: https://github.com/RUB-SysSec/PriDi ; https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3176258.3176338 — Certain on method; magnitudes not retrieved (ACM PDF 403) — Guessing on details.

**F7. Northeastern 2016 "Price Comparison" Chrome extension** (Lazer, Wilson, Radford, Knight-funded): compared user's price to server-fetched price on Amazon, Google Flights, Priceline Hotels; researchers reported "little evidence of price discrimination" but more steering. The Web Store listing now returns an empty page (delisted). https://news.northeastern.edu/2016/12/12/new-web-tool-helps-online-shoppers-detect-price-discrimination-on-e-commerce-sites/ — Certain (delisting: Likely).

**F8. 2020–2026 follow-ups are sparse and mostly not e-commerce audits.** Liu et al. 2024 (Chicago rideshare fairness): "data access and transparency hinder our ability to isolate discrimination from other factors" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.20522). FairFare 2025: crowdsourced 76,000+ trips from 45 drivers to estimate take rates (https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11273). Poort & Zuiderveen Borgesius 2025 (law/economics survey, no measurement) (https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.18212). A Verbraucherzentrale/German government study (2021) found "no evidence of personalised pricing for an overwhelming majority of retailers"; device-based differences on Amazon were attributed to dynamic pricing and presentation differences (http://recent-ecl.blogspot.com/2021/03/german-study-finds-no-evidence-of.html) — Likely.

## 2. Findings — investigative / consumer studies

**F9. Consumer Reports + Groundwork Collaborative + More Perfect Union, Instacart, published Dec 9, 2025.** Method: 437 volunteers recruited, 193 submissions kept after cleaning (179 online, 14 in-person); five tests in Sept 2025 at 2 Target and 3 Safeway stores (Seattle, Washington DC, Saint Paul, North Canton OH); researcher-set basket of 18–20 items; screenshots of carts captured in real time; Nov 2025 confirmation test with 88 volunteers across 15 retailers. Results: 74% of items showed multiple prices; max spread 23% (Signature Select Corn Flakes $2.99–$3.69); average spread 13% for varied items; ~7% basket-level; baskets ranged $114.34–$123.93 (Seattle Safeway) and $84.43–$90.47 (North Canton Target). "$1,200/yr" = Instacart's own $363/person/month family-of-four estimate ($17,424/yr) × 7%. Regressions on age, gender, race, income, usage frequency found differences "small and not statistically meaningful," i.e., the study found A/B-style randomization, not demographic targeting. Sources: https://github.com/consumerreports/instacart ; https://groundworkcollaborative.org/work/instacart/ — Certain.

**F10. Instacart's response.** Dec 22, 2025: "effective immediately, Instacart is ending all item price tests." Its rebuttal document says the tests were retailer-run "randomized A/B testing" by "only 10 retail partners," "the maximum '23 percent' figure… refers to a price difference of $0.70," and that an "extraordinarily high error rate… infected more than half the submissions." Instacart told CR it would still let retailers/CPG brands "test different types of promotions and discounts," and the NY AG's Jan 8, 2026 letter quotes Instacart that partners "may use behavioral data… to inform discounts or promotional offers." Sources: https://company.instacart.com/static/pdfs/consumer-reports-article.pdf ; https://ag.ny.gov/sites/default/files/letters/letter-to-instacart-on-pricing-practices-letters-2026.pdf ; https://www.consumerreports.org/money/questionable-business-practices/instacart-stops-ai-pricing-experiments-a1176475852/ — Certain. Critique that this was A/B testing not personalization: https://truthonthemarket.com/2025/12/10/instacart-didnt-read-your-mind-it-ran-an-a-b-test/ — Certain.

**F11. Uber/Lyft "29 prices for one route" — Consumer Reports, June 16, 2026.** 174 volunteers; 30 virtual routes across 17 states (15 Uber, 15 Lyft) plus 12 in-person tests in Portland, OR; March–April 2026; requests within six minutes, often the same minute. Kansas City: 55 volunteers checking one route saw 29 distinct prices (Lyft 18-mile trip <$31 to $65); all 30 routes had ≥2 price groupings; median low-to-high gap 42.4%; 12.4% of advertised discounts judged fictitious reference prices. CR's stated limits: did not control for driver supply, ETA, routing, traffic, pin-location precision, latency. Uber/Lyft deny personalizing base fares. https://www.consumerreports.org/money/questionable-business-practices/uber-lyft-different-prices-for-same-ride-and-fake-discounts-a1093538909/ — Certain.

**F12. ProPublica / Princeton Review (2015).** ZIP-code pricing of $6,600–$8,400 for the same online SAT course; Asian Americans nearly twice as likely to get the high price. https://www.propublica.org/article/breaking-the-black-box-when-algorithms-decide-what-you-pay — Certain. No Markup-specific personalized-pricing audit found.

**F13. Target app geofencing — KARE 11, Feb 2019.** Two-month test: 10 items rose $262 total when the phone crossed into the store (Samsung 55" TV $499.99 → $599.99 in the parking lot; switch at ~50 ft). Target then labeled every price "online" or "in-store." https://www.kare11.com/article/money/consumer/target-changes-app-after-kare-11-investigation/89-40ee0e76-9a0f-425d-93b0-b0eb89150f6c — Certain.

**F14. EU / UK.** European Commission 2018 mystery-shopping study (LE Europe/Deloitte/Ipsos): 160 sites, 8 member states, 4 sectors; "price differences were only observed in 6% of tests, and the median price difference observed was less than 1.6%," but personalised ranking on 61% of sites (92% airlines). https://commission.europa.eu/publications/consumer-market-study-online-market-segmentation-through-personalised-pricingoffers-european-union_en — Certain. Citizens Advice "A Price of One's Own" (2018) is about loyalty penalties in essential services (£4.1bn/yr), not online retail. No Which? personalised-pricing audit was found.

## 4. Why detection was never productized

- **Product identity/parsing does not scale.** Both Telefónica papers and IMC 2014 needed hand-written per-site parsers ("substantially different HTML markup… we write custom HTML parsers"); $heriff's authors called template parsing "non-scalable." Every site redesign breaks the tool.
- **Noise dominates signal.** IMC 2014's control-twin design exists because inventory churn, datacenter inconsistency, tax, and "unknown" sources produce differences with no personalization; the authors could only report aggregate rates, never flag one price. Vissers found airline fares change between queries one minute apart; the EU study found a median 1.6% difference in 6% of tests. A consumer tool that flags individual prices would mostly emit false positives.
- **A/B tests are indistinguishable from personalization at the individual level.** IMC 2014 identified Expedia/Hotels.com bucket-based steering; the Instacart dispute (74% of items varied, but zero demographic correlation) shows that even a 437-person crowd cannot separate randomized experiments from targeting without knowing the assignment rule.
- **Detection needs many synchronized personas or a large crowd.** Hannak needed 300 real users through a proxy plus purchase-history personas; CR needed 55 simultaneous volunteers on one Uber route to see 29 prices. Vantage-point proxies cost money and get rate-limited/blocked (Vissers ran sequentially to avoid it).
- **Findings were modest.** Documented discrimination was a $12 member discount, ~6% mobile differences, ~$0.70 grocery gaps. Academic groups (Northeastern 2016 extension "little evidence of price discrimination") had no compelling consumer pitch, and the tools ($heriff 2013, Northeastern 2016) were abandoned; the only current attempt, natural-price (2026), is a 0-star private beta whose README admits it is "useless until enough users report the same products." No VC-funded detection startup was found; commercial "price comparison" extensions compare across retailers, not across users.

## 5. Methodological lessons for a product

1. Run a control twin for every treatment (identical config, simultaneous query) and only call personalization when treatment-vs-treatment inconsistency exceeds twin-vs-twin noise, aggregated over many queries and days (IMC 2014 §3.1).
2. Time-align queries to the minute or better; airline and rideshare prices move within 60 s (Vissers; CR Uber/Lyft same-minute protocol).
3. Match on exact product identity (SKU/ASIN/store location + fulfillment mode), and capture the "original price" and discount separately — Instacart varied reference prices even when sale prices matched (NY AG letter).
4. Hold constant, or explicitly model: geography/tax/currency (Argentina 20%, MA intra-state), referrer (23% aggregator effect), device/OS/UA, logged-in status, cookies/history, cached prices, datacenter routing, and geofence location (Target).
5. Persona training must be deliberate (affluent vs. budget browsing histories, actual purchases) — passive fingerprint variation found nothing in 2012 and 2014.
6. Expect A/B buckets; test whether the assignment correlates with user attributes (regression as CR did) before claiming surveillance pricing; randomized variation is legal and common.
7. Sample sizes that produced defensible claims: 300 real users + control (IMC 2014), 193 clean submissions per basket (Instacart), 55 volunteers per route (CR rideshare), 130k queries/66 profiles (airlines, null result).
